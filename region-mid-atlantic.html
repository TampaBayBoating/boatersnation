New to Boating

New to boating? The 8 steps below, in the right order. No experience assumed.

1Get legal first

All four states on this coast have their own rule, and they genuinely differ. New York (Brianna’s Law): since Jan. 1, 2025, every operator of a motorized vessel — any age, PWC included — must hold a boating safety certificate; the course can be taken from age 10 and the certificate is good for life. New Jersey: a Boating Safety Certificate from a State Police–approved course is required to operate a power vessel — and on non-tidal waters you ALSO need an MVC boat license ($18, age 16+), which does not replace the certificate. Delaware: operators born on or after Jan. 1, 1978 must complete a boating safety course before operating a motorized boat or PWC, and PWC operators must be 14 or older with course proof. Pennsylvania: a PFBC Boating Safety Education Certificate is required for every PWC operator and for anyone born on or after Jan. 1, 1982 running a boat over 25 hp — good for a lifetime. Tap through to State Rules & Agencies for each state’s official links.

Life jackets for everyone, throwable cushion, fire extinguisher, flares, horn, kill-switch lanyard. See exactly what the Coast Guard requires.

→ 3Rent before you buy

Try different boats through rentals or a boat club before spending a dollar. Learn what you actually like on the water first.

→ 4Learn the markers

“Red, right, returning” — channel markers, no-wake signs, and who has the right of way. The rules that keep everyone safe.

→ 5Launch, dock & anchor

Ramp etiquette, docking without the drama, knots that hold, and anchoring that actually sticks.

→ 6Respect Mid-Atlantic waters

Cold water first: the Coast Guard counts water at 70°F or below as cold water that can cause hypothermia, and puts the average mid-Atlantic winter water temperature at about 53°F — cold enough to cause unconsciousness in about an hour — so dress for the water, not the air, and wear the life jacket. This is working water: New York Harbor is the busiest port on the East Coast, and the big ships in the channels cannot stop or turn for you — stay out of their way. And the inlets bite back: currents in the ocean inlets can reach 3 knots (NOAA), channels shoal and shift — the Coast Guard has reported hazardous shoaling at Barnegat Inlet’s entrance and the Army Corps dredges it — so check the latest Local Notice to Mariners and never trust last month’s track through an inlet. Local knowledge that saves props — and lives.

Check the marine forecast, tell someone your float plan, and monitor VHF channel 16. If the bay looks angry, stay at the dock.
